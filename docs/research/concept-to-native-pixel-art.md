A concept image is a target for interpretation, not a guaranteed production grid, model sheet, reusable rig, or complete set of consistent poses. A visually appealing generated concept may contain ambiguous or conflicting details when inspected at a smaller grid or from another angle. That is a **hypothesis to test on the actual reference**, not evidence that its generator is defective or that any specific detail is wrong. ## Direct visual inspection and feasibility preflight

**Observed in this research:** I viewed the approved close-up and the [v02/v03 head comparison](../../assets/previews/standing-v03/head-before-after.png). The close-up presents separate right-looking eyes with dark upper edges, a small smile, a partly visible ear, layered fringe, and a tied, fanned-out rear hair mass. In the comparison, v03 retains a recognizable tie and dark hair mass, while its much coarser head depicts facial features and hair shapes with fewer, larger blocks than the displayed reference. The comparison is useful for identity, but the image on its left is an isolated reference, and presentation scaling is not evidence that all three heads share a native grid or were authored at the same scale.

**Best supported explanation:** translating a richly detailed reference to the current deliberately simplified drawing requires choosing which shapes and relationships survive, and the current drawing's placement, proportions, contour design and shading choices visibly differ from the reference. The reference and candidate also appear at very different levels of detail in the comparison. It is plausible that pixel budget contributes to the mismatch, but no controlled same-grid redraw or fixed-display-size multi-resolution test here establishes a hard 128×192 ceiling or isolates resolution from construction choices. Do not attribute the difference to a model defect, an unknown original art pipeline, or resolution alone.

Before treating a concept as a pixel blueprint, inspect a lossless original at 100% and enlarged with nearest-neighbor: compare apparent block widths and edge steps across face, hair and scene; identify large illustrated shading planes, fine gradients or texture; look for soft edges, halos, irregular blocks or color speckle that *could* result from resampling or compression. Check dimensions and file provenance when available. A PNG file can preserve an already resampled or previously compressed image, but its format alone does not prove compression artifacts, and irregular appearance alone cannot identify a cause. The approved close-up shows both stepped details and comparatively soft, finely varied shading; this is an observation of appearance, not a diagnosis of how it was produced. Treat inferred grid sizes as hypotheses and manually translate shapes into the chosen native grid rather than assuming a faithful one-click reduction.

**Feature budget, as a proposal:** spend the head's scarce pixels first on silhouette and fringe, relative eye placement and rightward gaze, then cheek/jaw and the restrained smile, with the ear and pale tie providing identity cues. Represent rear hair with a few connected masses and selective highlights instead of every strand; simplify nose and skin gradients before sacrificing gaze or expression. Give each feature enough separation to survive the intended display size, and revise or omit marks that merge into noise. This is a priority order for testing, not a fixed pixel count or a claim that all features fit at every size. Inspect the original, native export and gameplay-sized result together and ask the user to judge the selected candidate.

## Source pixels, viewport and display size

A sprite's **native canvas** is its editable pixel grid. Drawn height is the occupied part of that grid, not necessarily the canvas height. The **viewport** determines how much of the world is visible; the final screen scale and camera determine how many display pixels the character occupies. Enlarging a sprite with nearest-neighbor sampling makes its existing pixels larger; it does not create new facial information. A bigger canvas offers room for newly authored details only if the drawing is actually revised at that resolution. It also changes relative pixel density unless the scene and camera are adjusted.

| Candidate | Status and purpose |
| --- | --- |
| 128×192, 184-pixel drawn height | Existing provisional baseline; compare the current character as recorded. No gameplay-scale conclusion yet. |
| 192×288 | 1.5× canvas dimensions and 2.25× pixel area; independent crop attempts now recorded below, without a controlled fidelity result. |
| 256×384 | 2× dimensions and 4× pixel area; independent crop attempts now recorded below, without a controlled fidelity result. |

These are canvas comparisons, not claims that either larger version looks better or is feasible at the desired production cost; this source research did not test either larger version. Check the eventual independent experiment record for its results. Pixel area is a theoretical storage/work surface, not a multiplier for actual effort: for example, one uncompressed full-canvas RGBA image would contain 98,304, 221,184, or 393,216 bytes respectively, before compression, layers, metadata, or unused space. Actual editing time, memory allocation and file sizes depend on representation and content and are unmeasured. Hold composition, pose, anchor, reference and apparent character height comparable; redraw rather than just scale the baseline. Compare face and silhouette at native size, enlarged by integer factors, and at the **same intended gameplay display size** against a representative background. Record viewport, camera zoom, screen dimensions, filtering and actual drawn bounds. If the target display size is smaller than a high-resolution drawing, downsampling can discard its extra information; test the actual result rather than assuming a benefit. The proportions of the approved woman and the readability of her eye direction, fringe, tote strap, legs and shoes are the relevant outcomes.

Godot documents that `viewport` stretch renders at the base viewport size before scaling, while `canvas_items` draws at target resolution and can produce uneven sprite-pixel scaling. Integer stretch can preserve even displayed pixel sizes, with unused space or cropping constraints depending on window and settings. Its pixel-art guidance suggests a viewport baseline as an example, not a required character size. [Godot: multiple resolutions](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html). Actual scene transforms, camera movement and display settings still need a local visual check.

## What to construct and inspect

**Artist evidence:** Derek Yu's [pixel-art tutorial](https://derekyu.com/makegames/pixelart.html) demonstrates outline cleanup, stepped curves, shading from a chosen light direction, and using a palette; the tutorial explicitly treats complete elimination of irregular steps as unrealistic. Pedro Medeiros's [artist-authored article index](https://saint11.art/pixel_art_articles/) lists lessons on clusters, shading, anti-aliasing and animation. The older face and clothing tutorial index linked in the prior local research redirected during this check, so its contents were not reverified. [Cure’s Pixel Joint tutorial](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299) discusses deliberate pixel control, clusters, anti-aliasing, noise and palettes; it allows shape tools and does not require placing every pixel individually. [Saint11’s consistency article](https://saint11.art/blog/consistency/) discusses scale, motion and mixing styles intentionally. Both were directly accessible on 27 October 2026. These are techniques and examples, not universal rules or evidence about this character's final quality.

**Working recommendations:**

1. Block the hair/head, open jacket and tee, wide jeans, shoes and offset tote as readable masses before fold marks. Compare head-to-body proportions and the gap between legs to the approved full-body reference. Use the silhouette and large value planes to clarify anatomy under clothing before adding seams.
2. Build intentional connected color shapes (clusters) for hair, cheek, garment planes and folds. Check contour step rhythms for distracting bumps while preserving purposeful asymmetry. A single pixel can carry an eye or fabric cue; isolated pixels are not inherently errors, but scattered accents that obscure form should be questioned.
3. Organize colors by material and light/shadow roles, preserving warm skin and the distinct olive, blue and cream masses. Use selective material-colored contours rather than making every seam equally dark. The recorded 45-color v01 palette is a prototype, not an approved maximum. Aseprite [color modes](https://www.aseprite.org/docs/color-mode/) document indexed palette behavior; a limited palette is an artistic choice, not a prerequisite for native art.
4. Treat anti-aliasing as a deliberate choice: selected intermediate-color pixels may soften an edge, while automatic smoothing or texture filtering can blur the intended clusters. Inspect against both dark and light backgrounds; avoid assuming all edge smoothing is forbidden. Favor major planes and purposeful fold accents over random texture or near-identical color noise.
5. For this face, compare the eye placement and rightward gaze, far-eye size, brows and mouth expression, cheek/jaw, fringe, partial ear coverage, tied-back hair and loose hair silhouette together. A single pixel can change the expression; inspect the whole head and full body at the actual viewing size as well as enlarged. The v03 requirements and mask are recorded in the [head experiment](../experiments/2026-10-26-head-v03/record.md); keep existing locked areas intact unless a new experiment explicitly expands its scope.
6. Preserve named layers, frames and construction operations, and demonstrate a constrained edit after reopening. Aseprite's [basics](https://www.aseprite.org/docs/basics/) describe layers, frames and cels; its [animation guide](https://www.aseprite.org/docs/animation/) describes timing, tags and onion skinning. Those features support a workflow, but a file extension alone does not establish practical editability or likeness.

## Cinematic presentation and Godot

In a [Polychroma Games developer AMA](https://www.reddit.com/r/NintendoSwitch/comments/1m0j796/ama_we_are_polychroma_games_the_studio_behind_the/), the director describes deliberate cinematic framing and lighting, mentions blur and global illumination in some Switch scenes, and says the game uses a custom approach rather than Godot's `Sprite3D` for its 2D-in-3D presentation. This is direct creator evidence about stated intent and selected implementation facts; it does **not** establish the exact pipeline, editor, source sprite dimensions, palette, shaders or animation technique. The user-provided screenshots and the prior [reference study](until-then-art-direction.md) are separate visual evidence. Adopt principles for an original scene, not another game's assets or presumed internals.

A small Godot scene can test character/background separation, a motivated light source, depth layers, contrast and camera framing before committing to a 3D environment. Godot documents [2D parallax](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/2d/2d_parallax.html) as layers moving at different speeds relative to the camera, and [2D lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.6/tutorials/2d/2d_lights_and_shadows.html) as viewport-resolution effects. In particular, nearest texture filtering alone does not make lighting pixel-sized: lighting and shadows are computed at viewport resolution. A lower-resolution viewport can give a blockier result, while higher-resolution light may be a deliberate aesthetic choice. Evaluate both in the intended engine version; these are documented capabilities, not a claim that any has been run here. The pale tee and tote need to remain distinct from a warm street light, and the face needs enough contrast without a fixed orange tint baked over the character.

**Proposed hybrid test, not implemented here:** keep the native character and environment artwork legible in neutral light; use a restrained [CanvasModulate](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_canvasmodulate.html) for scene ambience, a localized `PointLight2D` for the street lamp and optional `LightOccluder2D` shadows. Compare no effect, localized light and a modest screen-space grade on the same shot. [Godot’s lighting guide](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.6/tutorials/2d/2d_lights_and_shadows.html) documents these light and shadow nodes; [CanvasTexture](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_canvastexture.html) supports optional normal maps to vary light response. A normal map needs authoring and per-pose consistency and may not suit a flat stylized face; add it only if a comparison justifies it. Godot’s [screen-reading shader](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/shaders/screen-reading_shaders.html) mechanism permits custom post-processing, including a deliberately authored grade. Choose whether the grade affects UI and background separately, and test that skin, tee and tote retain their identities. Documented options do not establish the look, performance or actual *Until Then* implementation. Godot’s lighting documentation also gives an optional shader approach to snap light sampling to a grid; test it against smooth light and a low-resolution viewport rather than presume one is correct.

## Animation routes and feasibility

| Route | Evidence and tradeoff for this character |
| --- | --- |
| Frame-by-frame | Aseprite supports layers, cels and timings; [Godot sprite animation](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/2d/2d_sprite_animation.html) supports image sequences and sheets. Distinct drawings give direct control of the face, overlapping hair, loose jacket, wide legs and tote, but repeated poses and revisions can cost work. Existing still construction is a starting point, not proof an attack can be made reliably. |
| Cutout or skeletal | Godot documents [cutout parts, hierarchy and pivots](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/cutout_animation.html); its [2D skeleton guide](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/2d_skeletons.html) describes deformation but flags itself as not yet updated for Godot 4.7. Shared parts can make some pose changes reusable; rotations, joints, occlusion, strap attachment, wide fabric and facial turns need visual testing, potentially with replacement drawings. Reuse is not a guarantee of lower total effort. |
| 3D rendered to sprites | A model, rig, materials and animations provide a reusable source for directions and poses; rendering and pixel cleanup are separate work. As a production precedent, Motion Twin artist Thomas Vasseur [describes Dead Cells’ 3D-to-sprite workflow](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-): a model and skeleton, custom low-resolution render, key poses and frame/normal-map exports, with quick retakes but unresolved pixel flicker. That team’s tools, style and workload differ from this character; their result does not demonstrate this project’s feasibility. The attempted Godot 3D animation introduction URL was unavailable during this check. Modeling the likeness, hair, loose garments, tote and a suitable pixel appearance adds setup and skills; no local test or reliable cost comparison exists. |

**Assessment:** A locally editable still exists, so one frame-based key-pose attack is the most direct first experiment, not a final production commitment. The tote strap, loose hair, jacket and jeans make both consistency and overlapping motion meaningful challenges. Face direction, eye expression, hair tie and overlapping clumps must remain coherent across drawings, while jacket, jeans and tote move without disconnecting the strap. Each additional pose, sheet export and design revision can require checking and repairing multiple frames; neither frame count nor pixel area alone predicts the real cost. The current three-quarter standing pose does not settle combat facing, attack or camera. Establish those choices and anticipation, contact, follow-through and recovery before adding frames; inspect motion with effects hidden, then run it in Godot. Record both a localized visual change and a timing change, including time and failures when observable. Compare alternatives only if this proof exposes a specific quality or revision-cost gap; no source here proves which method will be cheapest or suitable for the full game. See the [motion guide](../art/motion-guide.md), [art-production workflow](../workflow/art-production.md), and [asset contract](../art/asset-contract.md) for acceptance and evidence boundaries.
